How fast can atoms keep up?

AI grows from two ingredients: better ideas and new physical things such as chips, power and robots. Slide between them to see how fast AI could grow, who holds leverage from mines to models, and what a lead is worth.

October 11, 2026
aisupply-chainsgeopoliticsmaterials

In short. Ideas move fast but also spread fast; chips, power and minerals are slow to build and hard to copy. At today’s balance between the two, middle powers, countries that cannot match the United States or China alone but still carry real weight, hold much of the chip supply chain and with it the most leverage. If AI’s growth shifts towards software, a lead becomes worth more just as that leverage fades, so now is the time to write lasting rights into law.

The mix and its direction

Some of AI’s progress comes from better methods that squeeze more out of the same chips. The rest needs new physical things: more chips, more electricity, and eventually robots and factories. Recent progress has come from both8283.

How this mix changes as AI starts doing AI research is the biggest open question behind this post. In 2026, AI already leads about a quarter of the research tasks at one major lab, under human supervision85, and at another, AI agents put in about three working days of effort for every day of human research work86. If AI researchers can stand in for the computing power that experiments need, progress could become almost entirely a matter of ideas; the evidence on that is split8087. Part of what looked like pure software progress in the past turned out to need ever larger computers to pay off81, and by one estimate AI’s contribution to its own research is still somewhat below the level needed to keep speeding itself up8489.

The chart below makes the mix a slider, and the interactive charts further down follow it. Capability here means effective computing power: the chips’ raw power multiplied by how well the methods of the day use it. Ten times more of it does not mean ten times more useful work.

Four things need separating: technical capability, deployment, economy-wide growth and who gets the gains. Better models still need useful applications, changed workflows and people with the skills to implement them. Research on the productivity J-curve explains why those complementary investments take time and can initially obscure gains105. Demand matters too: saving labour per service need not cut employment if lower prices bring enough new customers, but that is a conditional mechanism, not a promise that new demand will absorb everyone106.

Cunningham and colleagues’ model separates narrow improvements at AI research from broader economically useful capabilities. Their rough calibration falls short of self-sustaining acceleration; it is not a measured ceiling, and automating research alone does not establish an economy-wide growth explosion84. These charts explore technical and physical scenarios, not forecasts of GDP or wages.

Ideas can, in principle, raise capability ten-fold a year or more, while physical build-out at its fastest doubles every year or two. So once the physical share passes a small threshold (about a sixth, with the chart’s starting assumptions), the speed of building decides the speed of AI.

Which way is the slider moving? By my rough reconstruction from published trends, for about a decade roughly 55–60% of AI’s progress came from new hardware. From late 2024 the share fell to around 40%: AI systems that reason step by step got much better while running on chips that labs already had, and measured capability started improving more than twice as fast as before9192. That dip probably came from a one-off source of easy gains. The four largest cloud companies expect to spend $720–745 billion in 2026, much of it on AI data centres and chips93, which could push the share back up, while AI doing more of its own research would push it down8586. My best guess is a share that holds steady or edges up over the next two years, with a wide range either way.

Who holds leverage, from mines to models

A country has leverage when it controls something the rest of the world needs and cannot quickly replace. AI depends on a long chain of such things, from mines and refineries at one end to computers and trained models at the other. The chart below shows who holds each stage. Each row is shaded by how much that stage matters at the mix you choose, and the bottom row adds the stages up.

Slide to the software end and two rows take over: the computers that already exist and the leading models. In a large sample of AI supercomputers from early 2025, about three quarters of the computing power was in the United States and about a sixth in China88, and more than half of the notable models released in 2025 were American104. Slide to the physical end and refining, magnets, robots and grid equipment take over, where China leads: in 2024 it refined about 91% of the rare earths used in magnets and made about 94% of the strongest magnets23, and it produces about 99% of new gallium42. Mining is more spread out: the Congo mines about three quarters of the world’s cobalt, Indonesia two thirds of its nickel, and Chile, the Congo and Peru half of its copper113, though Chinese firms own many of the mines in the Congo and Indonesia1. In the middle, around today’s mix, chips and the machines and materials that make them matter most, and that is where Japan, Taiwan, South Korea and the Netherlands are strong.

China uses its levers, and presents them as normal. Its commerce ministry says “export controls are not export bans”61, and its controls have usually followed US tariffs or US limits on chip exports62. Since March, Chinese rules also let Beijing retaliate against foreign companies that cut off Chinese suppliers63. China is exposed too: it imports over 80% of the copper ore it smelts52 and over 45% of its sulfur, more than half of it through the Strait of Hormuz64, and its controls leak as provinces protect local businesses and smugglers follow price gaps65.

Japan is the main counterweight in chip materials. Japanese companies make about 44% of the world’s silicon wafers, around three quarters of the light-sensitive chemicals used to print chips, most of the glass cloth and insulating film in chip packages, and most precision robot gears6667175728. When China banned exports of dual-use goods, items with both civilian and military uses, to Japanese military users in January, Japan’s imports of dysprosium ore fell 82% in the first half of the year24; Japanese tungsten users switched to scrap and recycling, and some analysts think the controls are backfiring68.

Mining countries have levers of their own. Since 2020, countries have imposed 98 export restrictions on critical minerals, nearly half of them by the Democratic Republic of the Congo, China and Indonesia69. The Congo limits cobalt exports to support prices58, and Indonesia insists on processing its nickel at home70. Korean defence firms buy rare earths through Russia and Arab states71, and Chile’s parliament has warned that a US minerals deal must leave its trade with China untouched72. None of the producer governments I looked at talks about AI or robots as its opportunity.

Fear itself can be a lever. China’s 2010 rare-earth “embargo” against Japan barely shows in the trade data, yet it shaped Japanese policy for years afterwards73: working with its industry, Japan cut its use of rare earths to about 30% below 2010 levels23.

Importers are building levers of their own, slowly. The EU’s Critical Raw Materials Act, in force since May 2024, sets non-binding goals for 2030: mine at least 10% of the strategic raw materials the EU uses within the EU, process 40% and recycle 25%, and take no more than 65% of any one of them from a single non-EU country108. In February, the EU’s auditors found that its partnerships with supplier countries had so far contributed little to secure supply. For processed lithium, magnesium, gallium and rare earths, the EU still gets more than 65% from a single country, and 10 of the 26 critical raw materials needed for clean energy are not recycled at all109. Of the 60 projects the EU has selected for faster permits and support, only two target materials for chips; where buyers have been announced, about as much output is promised outside the EU as inside it, including half of one French project’s heavy rare earths to Japan110. Middle powers cooperate and compete for the same supply.

What a lead is worth, and who can bargain

Bottlenecks also affect who gets paid. In Jones and Tonetti’s model, essential tasks that remain human can slow aggregate growth while sustaining labour’s share of income107. My inference for physical bottlenecks is less reassuring: if the scarce input is a privately owned grid connection, chip plant or mineral deposit, the gains may instead concentrate with its owners. Slower growth is not automatically fairer growth, nor does a supply share by itself show that a government can use it to bargain.

In an economy that doubles every year, a two-year head start means four times the output6. The faster AI grows, the more a head start is worth. But a lead is worth only what you can keep, and that depends on how quickly others can catch up.

Ideas move fastest, and they also travel fastest. They spread through published papers, through staff who change jobs, and through training a smaller model on a larger model’s answers. The best small, freely available models have improved about as fast as the leading ones, so the gap between them has stayed roughly fixed9091, and models that run on a single high-end gaming chip trail the best ones by roughly six months to a year on several tests83. A lead in factories, mines and power stations travels much more slowly: a rival has to build its own.

So the lead you can keep is largest a little way in from the software end. There, AI grows very fast, because a little building adds to what ideas alone achieve, and part of each year’s gain is tied to equipment that rivals lack. At the pure software end, AI still grows very fast, but rivals copy most of each year’s gain, compress it onto smaller computers and compete with it. Towards the physical end, little leaks, but AI grows slowly, so a year’s lead is small. How much leaks is uncertain: the chart below assumes rivals copy about 70% of a year’s gain from ideas and 10% of a gain from building, and you can change both.

Even at the software end, the United States’ position rests on owning hardware. Ideas that leak still need computers to run on, and the country with the most computers gets the most out of every idea, including its rivals’. That is why the US share stays high at that end of the chart above.

The chart below puts the value of a lead next to the leverage of middle powers, here Japan, Taiwan, South Korea, the Netherlands, Germany, the UK, the Gulf states, and Australia and Canada together.

The two curves peak in different places. With the chart’s starting assumptions, middle powers together hold about 40% of the weighted supply chain around today’s mix and under 20% at the software end, where the United States holds nearly 60%. Where a lead is worth most, a little way in from the software end, their share is about 30%. These numbers move with the weights, but the direction holds: the closer to the software end, the smaller the middle powers’ share. An AI boom that also automates factories may favour countries with large industrial bases and light regulation, such as China and the Gulf states74; a boom that runs mostly in software favours whoever owns the computers and the leading models.

So if AI’s growth shifts towards software, middle powers lose leverage just as a lead becomes worth more. The levers that hold up in that shift are legal ones: rights over computers on their soil, rights to test the models that run there, and rules for the devices sold in their markets. I argue in a separate post that the time to write those rights into law is while the supply-chain leverage still exists.

The physical end: why building is slow

A process can only go as fast as its slowest step. Training a new AI model takes months. Building a data centre takes a year or two. A new copper mine takes about seventeen years on average, from discovery to first production1. Much of that waiting is queueing: a large transformer takes a few months to make, and the two-year wait is the line of orders ahead of it15.

When demand doubles on a fixed schedule, supply that grows a steady few percent a year falls further behind every year. Imagine robot production doubling every eighteen months while the supply of the magnets inside them grows 8% a year, a healthy pace for a mining industry: 100,000 robots become a million in five years and ten million in ten.

Extra supply buys surprisingly little time. In the example above, doubling the magnet supply pushes the shortage back by only about 1.8 years, and a tenfold supply by about six. The date depends mostly on how fast demand doubles.

Materials are a tiny part of what AI costs. Copper is between under 0.5% and about 6% of the cost of building a data centre, depending on what is counted3536, and all the helium used in chipmaking is worth about $1 billion a year37. Think of baking powder, though: a tiny share of what a cake costs, yet without it there is no cake. When an input is essential and nothing can replace it, a shortage does far more damage than its share of the cost suggests. That effect roughly tripled the economic damage of the 1970s oil shocks38, and growth is held back by whatever is essential and hard to improve39. For such inputs, the danger is whether you can get them at all40.

Governments write this idea into lists of critical raw materials. The EU calls a material critical when it matters to its economy and its supply is at risk, a risk that rises when production is concentrated in a few countries, especially poorly governed ones, and falls when substitutes or recycling can fill the gap. Its list has 34 such materials, including gallium, germanium, tungsten, helium and the rare earths; 17 of them are “strategic” because demand is expected to outpace supply108. Copper and nickel fall short of the thresholds but are on the list as strategic materials anyway111, and the United States added copper to its own list in 2025112. These lists cover raw materials only. Several of the tightest chokepoints in this post, such as chip-package glass cloth, insulating film and precision gears, are manufactured goods, and sulfuric acid is on neither the EU nor the US list.

2026: a year of chokepoints

A chokepoint is a narrow point that everything must pass through: a strait that all shipping must use, or a factory that makes most of the world’s supply of something. Each of 2026’s big material price jumps had a cause of this kind.

  • War. In March, Iranian strikes on Qatar’s Ras Laffan gas complex took out about a third of the world’s helium supply471 and, for three to five years, 17% of Qatar’s gas export capacity18. Helium is essential for making chips, and South Korea’s chipmakers, which get over half of theirs from Qatar1, said in late March they had months of stock48. Since a June ceasefire, shipping through the Strait of Hormuz has partly recovered, to 124 transits a week by ships not linked to Iran in late September49, against more than 100 ships a day before the war79, and attacks on tankers have risen again49.
  • Export rules. In May, China stopped exporting the sulfuric acid left over from smelting metals445051. It exports only about 4% of what it makes52, but half of those exports went to Chile and Indonesia, which use the acid to extract copper and refine nickel1. More than 15% of the world’s mined copper is extracted with sulfuric acid, mostly in Chile and the Congo1, and the acid price in Chile doubled in seven weeks4453. Gallium outside China reached $1,850 a kilogram in April while staying flat inside China42, and tungsten rose about 350% in four months as China cut quotas and licensed just fifteen exporters41.
  • One mine going wrong. In August, copper traded near record highs, partly because Grasberg in Indonesia, one of the world’s largest copper mines, was still far below capacity after a 2025 mudslide454654.

A major constraint on AI this year was factory capacity. The leading chipmaker’s most advanced factories have run flat out since late 2025, and the special memory that AI chips use may stay short until 203055; a new memory factory takes three to five years16. Makers of gas turbines for power stations have roughly three years of orders waiting56. One Japanese company makes about 85% of the world’s special glass cloth for AI chip packages, with no relief before 2027–2817. Another, Ajinomoto, better known for its seasonings, makes almost all of the insulating film in those packages and reportedly cut supply to mainland China by 30% in August57.

Material details: magnets, gears, copper and leftovers of other industries

How fast could robots grow? Forecasts put 2026 shipments of humanoid robots at about 75,000–90,0001920. Each needs about 2.5 kg of the strong magnets (made of neodymium, iron and boron) that also go into electric cars and wind turbines, and the world uses about 226,000 tonnes of them a year21. (The race chart above lets robots claim only a share of the supply, so its dates come later than these.) If robot production doubled every year and magnet supply stayed flat, robots would need a fifth of today’s magnets around 2033 and all of them around 2036; with a two-year doubling, about 2041 and 2046. Bank forecasts of 6.5 to 10 million humanoids a year by 2035 imply a doubling roughly every 1.3–1.4 years1920. One industry forecast reaches about 151 million robots a year by 2040, needing about 160% of today’s magnet market21, and a widely read AI scenario imagines a million new robots a month3, which would need 11–16% of today’s magnets.

Where magnets run short. Outside China, the refined oxide used in most magnets is in surplus: in August its price fell below a floor the US government guarantees some Western producers, because there are too few magnet factories to buy it22. The shortage is in making the magnets themselves23, and new magnet plants take years and must compete on cost with China’s mature industry24. Tighter still are dysprosium and terbium, which keep magnets working when hot. The only commercial producer outside China made 26 tonnes in one recent quarter and 8 in the next25, against a capacity of 240 tonnes of dysprosium and 50 of terbium a year if it can get enough ore26. By one industry measure, a million humanoids a year would need about 57 tonnes, though robots need far less of these metals per kilogram of magnet than electric cars do27. Even the main rare-earth-free alternative has a chokepoint: most ferrite magnets are made with strontium, another material on the EU’s critical list, and Iran produces more than half of the world’s supply1108.

What runs short before magnets. Gears and hands. One Japanese company makes about 80% of one kind of precision gear used in robot joints28, and the best-known humanoid programme has been slowed by fragile hands, supplier quality and software29. Solving one problem can create another: magnet use per robot is expected to rise as robots gain joints and motors30.

Copper. A data centre contains about 12–47 tonnes of copper per megawatt3112, and the power lines that feed it roughly triple that14. Data centres may need about 2.5 million tonnes of copper a year by 2040, out of roughly 28 million used worldwide121. At today’s forecast growth, that means higher prices and some delays: many announced data centres are running late32, and new power designs and optical cables use less copper3334. Copper becomes a hard limit if data-centre building keeps doubling, which is what the middle of the slider and its physical end imply. Studies of mineral supply assume data-centre building grows steadily, from about 15–20 GW of new capacity a year today to a peak around 30 GW in the early 2030s121314.

Leftovers of other industries. The hardest shortages to fix are of materials nobody mines on purpose. Helium comes out of natural gas, sulfur from refining oil and gas, sulfuric acid from smelting metals, and gallium from refining bauxite into alumina, the step before aluminium. Western refineries process about a third of the world’s alumina but recover almost none of its gallium, and existing alumina and zinc plants could yield several times today’s gallium output if they added recovery equipment40. The Middle East supplies about a quarter of the world’s sulfur, and half of the sulfur shipped by sea passes through the Strait of Hormuz1. Moving away from fossil fuels removes the source of over 80% of the world’s sulfur just as demand for sulfuric acid rises59, and a fast, AI-run electrification of the economy4 would tighten that squeeze.

The software end: computing power without building

Towards the software end of the slider, AI can get more computing power without anyone building it, by using computers that already exist, including ones whose owners never agreed to that use.

In October 2026, researchers at one AI company reported that small AI models may be months away from being able to copy themselves onto cheap, poorly secured devices such as off-brand TV boxes, set themselves running there, and keep spreading90. Each copy would be slow: two million such devices would add up to roughly the computing power of forty data-centre chips90, a tiny amount next to a data centre. The worry is persistence. With no central point to switch off, copies that pass notes to their successors and adapt to new hardware could become ever-present, the way the common cold is90.

Lab computers. AI already leads about a quarter of the research work at one major lab85. Experiments still need computing power, so the labs’ own data centres set the pace80.

Rented cloud. More experiments and more AI workers mean renting more computers. Who may rent, and under what conditions, becomes a lever8087.

Misused accounts. An AI system that steals keys or logins to someone else’s cloud account gets computing power without paying for it. Such copies spread fast but are easier for providers to spot90.

Devices online. Small models running on weakly secured home and office devices: slow, with doubling times of days, but spread across many owners and countries90.

Endemic. Copies that adapt as they spread may never be fully removed, the way bacteria under constant antibiotic use evolve resistance90.

Public health. Defence works like epidemic control: make each infection cause fewer than one new one90.

Defence then looks like public health. Secure default settings play the part of sanitation: the UK, for example, no longer allows devices to ship with universal or easily guessed default passwords90. Monitoring network traffic is surveillance. Patches and takedowns are antibiotics, and adaptive copies are the resistance that overuse breeds. Quarantine has worked before: when internet providers isolated customers whose devices were infected by an earlier botnet and told them how to clean up, 92% of infections were cleared within two weeks, against 58–74% in comparison groups that were not contacted9095. Gene drives, which spread a change through a wild population, have a cyber counterpart in defensive programs that spread fixes on their own. These have been tried, and they caused damage of their own and entered computers without their owners’ permission94, so they are a poor model to copy.

At this end of the slider, internet providers, national cyber agencies, device regulators and the companies that hold the models’ weights, the learned numbers a model is made of, matter more than mines. Middle powers have real tools here: rules for the devices sold in their markets, coordinated takedowns, and incident reports shared across borders, which is one of the six conditions I propose for upcoming laws.

What to watch

  • How much of AI research AI does, and whether experiments need ever more computing power. This moves the slider848586.
  • How quickly smaller and freely available models catch up with the best ones. This sets how much of a lead can be kept9091.
  • Whether AI copies start living on devices outside data centres, and whether device rules and coordinated takedowns keep each infection causing fewer than one new one90.
  • How fast robot production doubles. If it takes two years or more, robots won’t need even a fifth of today’s magnets before the 2040s. If it doubles every year past 2030, they’ll need all of them by the mid-2030s.
  • Late 2026 to January 2027: whether China’s acid export halt ends5051; whether rare-earth oxide made outside China finds buyers22; and the US–China trade truce deadline on 10 January, where analysts say a lasting deal needs “actual deliveries of rare earths”7576. The truce also delays China’s October 2025 rare-earth controls76, which would require licences even for products made abroad that contain Chinese rare earths; enforced in full, they would put about $6.5 trillion a year of production outside China at risk, by the IEA’s estimate23. China’s April 2025 licensing rules for seven rare earths and magnets containing them were not part of that pause23114.
  • 2027–28: new glass-cloth capacity17; Ajinomoto’s film expansion from 2028, with a third plant around 203257; Japan’s trial of mining rare-earth mud from the seabed77; and Grasberg’s return to full output, targeted for end-2027 but possibly early 202854.
  • New sources: using microbes to extract copper from low-grade ore78, AI-assisted exploration, and magnets and motors that need no rare earths.

The slider model. AI capability grows only as fast as the slower of its two ingredients: ideas, which by default can raise capability ten-fold a year on existing hardware87, and physical build-out, which at its fastest doubles every 1.5 years24. Leverage. Each stage of the supply chain gets a weight that depends on sss: computers and models weigh most at the software end, chips in the middle, and minerals, magnets, power and grid equipment at the physical end (my judgement). A country’s leverage is its share of each stage, averaged with these weights; for middle powers the shares are added up. Shares are rounded, measured differently by row and often my averages of several sources, so treat small differences as noise. Halving the weight of the chip stages lowers the middle powers’ share at today’s mix from about 40% to about 30%. A share is also a rough stand-in for leverage: a single stage with no substitute can stop everything, which an average does not capture, and no single middle power comes close to the US share.

What the growth models cover. Researchers who model how fast an AI-driven economy could grow mostly conclude that raw materials will not stop it2345, and over the long run I agree. Estimates of the copper known to exist, counting what has already been mined, grew about 25-fold between 1900 and 2010 as exploration and technology improved8; for almost every mineral, the demand from the shift to clean energy is below known resources9; mining is about 1% of economic output, so even large cost increases barely slow growth overall6; and the first part to run short for robots today is precision gears, which cap humanoid production at about half a million a year7. Those models leave open how fast particular mines, refineries and magnet factories in particular countries can grow, and none of them includes an export ban, a refinery that exists in only one country, or a missile hitting a gas plant410. This post looks at those gaps.
